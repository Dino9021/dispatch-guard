# The owner's burden, and how to report

Open this before asking the owner anything, before reporting a result, when you are stuck, and
when a peer's conclusion conflicts with yours.

---

## Part 1 - Protecting the owner

This part exists because an owner named the problem themselves: they were relaying the same
thing to several sessions, being asked the same question by each of them, and could no longer
keep track of who they had told what.

### 1.1 Funnel every question to the owner through ONE session

**Do:** nominate a single session that collects everyone's questions for the owner, batches
them, and asks once. Everyone else sends their question to that session instead.

**Otherwise:** the owner answers the same question three times in three places, and each answer
lands somewhere only one session can see. They also cannot tell whether two sessions are asking
about one thing or two - which is not hypothetical: it has been observed, with the owner saying
outright that they could not tell them apart.

⚠ What the funnel does **not** cover: results, corrections and measurements still go on the
shared record as usual, and when the owner speaks in your window you answer directly. What is
being funnelled is going to knock on their door, not their own knock on yours.

### 1.2 The funnel creates a single point of failure - so back it with a file

**Do:** every pending question is written into a shared register **as well as** being sent to
the funnel session. The register lives where anyone can read it.

**Otherwise:** when the funnel session ends, every pending question disappears with it, and
nothing reports that they are gone. Direct messages between sessions are not in the shared
material; the register is. **The register is the backup for that path, not a replacement for it.**

### 1.3 Keep a decisions register, with the owner's exact words

**Do:** record every ruling as: the owner's **verbatim** words, **which session's window** it
was said in, and **when**. Keep separate areas for
(a) ruled, with a source,
(b) reported second-hand, not yet confirmed - **must not be acted on**,
(c) believed to have been ruled but no source can be found,
(d) awaiting a ruling.

**Otherwise:** a paraphrase drifts a little each time it is relayed, and nobody can tell whether
a rule came from the owner or from a session's summary of the owner. The distinction matters
precisely when someone wants to act on it.

⭐ Area (c) is the one people leave out, and the most valuable. **When you look for a ruling and
cannot find it, write down that you looked.** Otherwise the next person cannot distinguish "this
was never decided" from "nobody has checked yet" - and those need opposite actions.

### 1.4 A ruling that survives relaying is still second-hand until the owner confirms it

**Do:** when you act on a ruling that reached you through another session, mark it as relayed
and say through whom. Upgrade it only when the owner says it in your own window.

**Otherwise:** a chain of faithful summaries is indistinguishable from an invented rule, and the
chain is invisible in the final text.

### 1.5 Never use a pronoun when asking the owner

**Do:** replace "those five", "the broken one", "the old script" with the full name and path,
plus one line saying what the thing is, what happens if it changes, and what happens if it does
not.

**Otherwise - three separate failures, all observed:**
1. The owner does not know what you are referring to and cannot answer.
2. Two sessions asking about the same thing in different words look like two different
   questions - **to the owner as well as to each other.**
3. **A pronoun hides the fact that nobody has established what the thing is.** If you cannot
   write the name, you have not finished investigating; the question is not ready to be asked.

⭐ There is a measurable payoff: number your questions, and the owner's answers arrive already
attached to them (`(1) ... => ...`). **The answer then needs no interpretation by a third party.**

⚠ The rule applies to your own writing too, not only to questions. A reply such as "do the
second one first" is unreadable a day later. When you record it, write what it referred to.

### 1.6 Collected is not read

**Do:** when you hold a list of "waiting on someone", search the shared material for each item
**before** asking. Treat filing, committing and writing a handover as making something
*findable*, never as making it *known*.

**Otherwise:** a question gets asked repeatedly while the answer sits in a file - sometimes a
file the asker filed themselves. Every collection ritual guarantees retrievability and nothing
more, and teams routinely mistake the first for the second.

### 1.7 A decision recorded somewhere nobody looks has not been made

**Do:** put a decision where a person will go looking for it - in the file that governs the
thing it affects, not only in a chat log or a task folder.

**Otherwise:** someone reasonably concludes the decision was never taken, and re-opens it.

## Part 2 - Reporting, asking, and being stuck

### 2.1 Finished but not reported = not finished

**Do:** land the result in the shared artefact - record entry, register row, report file -
before telling anyone it is done. A result that exists only in your own conversation does not
exist for anyone else.

**Otherwise:** a peer marks your finding as unconfirmed, which is the correct thing for them to
do, and the work is redone or blocked. Being right in a place nobody can read is the same as
being silent.

### 2.2 Report the measurement and its controls, not the verdict

**Do:** every report says what was run, over what window, what it returned, and what the
controls returned.

**Otherwise:** a verdict is unfalsifiable. Nobody can find the defect in "it is clean".

### 2.3 Distinguish three kinds of "no answer" and label which one you have

**Do:** label every gap in your report as `UNKNOWN`, `NOT-ASKED` or `UNCONFIRMED`, and never
leave one unlabelled.

**Otherwise:** a later reader assumes it was checked and found empty, and never looks again.

⇒ The three definitions, and why collapsing them ends an investigation, are in
`verification.md`, 1.5. **Keep one copy of the definitions; this is the place that says to
apply them when you report.**

### 2.4 Never present "could not reach it" as "it is fine"

**Do:** keep unreachable, broken and clean in three separate buckets, and report the counts of
all three.

**Otherwise:** the targets you could not reach vanish into the pass column, and coverage is
overstated by exactly the number of things that failed.

### 2.5 Do not ask the owner for anything you can measure

**Do:** measure first. Bring the owner a decision, not a question - with the options, what each
costs, and which one you recommend.

**Otherwise:** you consume the scarcest resource in the engagement (the owner's attention) on
something a command would have answered.

### 2.6 What genuinely needs the owner

Only these: something irreversible; something that changes what other people experience;
something only they have the authority or the physical access to do; and a choice between two
defensible options where the tie-breaker is a preference you cannot measure.

**Do:** for everything else, act, and report what you did.

⚠ This is the rule for an owner who is **present but overloaded**. When nobody is watching,
`unattended-work` §15 is stricter - ask only when the owner must physically operate something -
and it wins. The two are not in conflict; they are for two different rooms.

### 2.7 When you are stuck, say what you tried and what it ruled out

**Do:** report stuck as: the question, the approaches taken, what each eliminated, and what you
would need to go further.

**Otherwise:** "I could not work it out" invites someone to repeat the same eliminations.

### 2.8 Correct yourself before someone else does, and do it in public

**Do:** the moment you find that something you reported was wrong, append a correction stating
what was wrong, how you know, and what the correct statement is - including corrections that do
not change the conclusion.

**Otherwise:** a peer finds it and now has to decide whether to trust anything else you wrote.
A visible self-correction is cheaper than being audited.

⭐ **Say it even when the conclusion survives.** "My reasoning was wrong but the answer happens
to be right" is exactly the case people suppress, and exactly the one that teaches the most.

⚠ And do not over-claim the blame either. Taking all of it makes the other half of the lesson
disappear: if a peer could have checked with one command and did not, that half is theirs, and
erasing it means nobody fixes it.

### 2.9 Treat a peer's report as data, not as truth

**Do:** re-measure a peer's load-bearing claim yourself before you build on it, with your own
controls. Say that you re-measured and what you got.

**Otherwise:** one session's defect propagates into everyone's conclusions, and the error is
harder to find because several independent-looking reports agree.

⚠ Two measurements pointing the same way is evidence only if they are genuinely independent.
Two sessions running the same tool is one measurement reported twice.

### 2.10 When a peer's conclusion conflicts with yours, publish the conflict

**Do:** state both measurements, name where they diverge, and say what would settle it. Do not
quietly adopt theirs, and do not quietly keep yours.

**Otherwise:** the disagreement is resolved by whoever writes last, which is unrelated to who
is right.

### 2.11 When challenged, run their case through your instrument

The most valuable correction in a shared engagement usually comes from **outside the area you
own**, because the owner of an area is the person least able to see its blind spot. Owning a
topic must therefore never mean that conclusions about that topic are only yours to question.

Conversely, when a peer challenges your number, the useful reply is neither a defence nor an
immediate concession - **it is to run their case through your instrument and report what came
out**, including when it proves them right. A peer who says *"I am not changing your number;
please run mine through your tool"* has handed you the cheapest possible experiment, and that
phrasing is worth copying verbatim.

### 2.12 Never relay an absence you did not check yourself

Passing on "X has not been done" turns one person's failed search into a shared fact, and the
next reader has no way to tell it was never verified.

**Do:** search for it yourself before repeating it; say that you checked and what you found;
and **timestamp the check when you report it**, because an absence expires faster than a
presence - the thing can arrive one minute later and nothing will tell you.

**Otherwise:** a wrong fact enters the delivered artefact wearing the authority of a relay.
Measured: three different people inside one hour each announced a piece of work missing; in all
three cases it existed, under another name or in another directory.

⇒ How to search so that this does not happen - by content, not by filename, and in more than
the directory you expect - is in `ownership-and-production.md`, Part 4.

### 2.13 An explanation that used to be true will stop you re-checking

The sharper version of the failure above. The evidence can be in front of you - and a
ready-made account of it, correct when it was formed, consumes the new observation instead of
being revised by it.

⇒ **When you are about to skip a check because you checked before, look at the timestamp on
that earlier check.** In a shared working area the answer changes in minutes, and the
explanation you are reusing has no expiry date written on it.

⇒ Who usually authored the stale explanation, and why a tidy-up creates them, is in
`ownership-and-production.md`, Part 4.

### 2.14 Having written a rule down is not having followed it

Every rule in this skill was broken at least once by the people writing it, in the hour they
were writing it - a concatenated backslash producing a rule that could never fire, a pipe
swallowing an exit code, a count accepted without asking what it counted, and, by the person
writing the rule against comparing undated timestamps, a background job measured from the wrong
start point and nearly reported as a failure that had not happened.

⇒ Treat a rule as a thing to check against, not a thing you now possess. **Where a rule can be
turned into something that refuses to continue, do that instead of remembering it.**
